I recently completed a very successful agile software development engagement.  I have been hearing lately that "agile is dead", and I beg to differ.  Here are my numbers.

This project was a legacy code refresh engagement, but  when I came on board as Project Manager it was a rewrite project.  The legacy technology was a workflow application for case management written in VB.Net circa 2002.  The chosen platform for the rewrite was Glassfish.  There were four developers, two business analysts, two subject matter experts, and two key stakeholders.  There was some mock web service development in progress, and there was a business requirements document in progress.  They had 3 months to deliver a host of new Program changes to their customer in order to meet contractual commitments.  It was very clear that the current approach was not going to meet requirements.

We immediately called a pause on the rewrite, and performed a quick survey of the legacy environment.  On my recommendation we abandoned the rewrite approach for a refresh of the current system, and we launched into firing up an agile software development team.  Our refresh strategy was not to upgrade the .Net framework to the latest version, but to stay at the current .Net 2.0'ish level and not to make any fundamental changes to the architecture of the application.  Since this was a cut-over project, as compared to a parallel implementation, the data requirements were quite challenging.  We could not make changes to the existing data model that would break any workflow items currently in flight.

The legacy system was a typical .Net workflow application from around 2002.  It had the typical granular security system very common to enterprise application architectures at that time.  All "state" was stored in a very document-like data model, so there were no queues or other moving parts.  There was a bit of a billing system welded to the core workflow application that should be factored out, but the application in general was not causing them issues.   The application had a connection to an external system using good-old-fashioned SOAP web services, and changes were made to both ends of this interface.  There was one monthly batch job that extracted data, and there were about 30 reports that were in a neglected state of repair.

Since we had no .Net developers on our team (the original developers had moved on), we initiated the first of two rounds of hiring.  We brought on 10 .Net developers, and at launch we were a team of 13 developers (3 Java 10 .Net) , 1 BA, 2 SME's, and two stakeholders.  The Java developers filled the roles of interface development, build automation, and reporting.  The .Net developers were, of course, focused on legacy code changes.

We spent about 10 calendar days getting the team in place and planning our approach.  We did our sticky note workshop and came out with 185 tasks.   Our requirements were to complete a re-branding release in 2 months, and the full feature release in 3 months.  I have used one-month sprints in the past with success (3 weeks development, 1 week technical debt), but we ended up planning for a two-month sprint for the re-branding release and a three-month sprint for the full release.  Both sprints would be run concurrently, and our development team was focused on both sprints at the same time.  The re-branding release included some lightweight branding and wording changes to the current system, and having this quick release allowed us to validate our approach early in the schedule.

Our two sprints included very little technical debt.  We really did not have the runway for any nice-to-have changes.  We had one period of user acceptance testing for each release.  The brand release UAT took about 5 weeks and the full production UAT was 3 weeks.  Our testing approach was very "old school".  We did not have any unit testing in the code given the era it hailed from, and we did not have the room in our schedule to take on TDD.  We also did not have any resources to dedicate to test automation of any kind.  Our testing was performed by our SME's after our developers completed each change, and we relied on our experts to tell us if the software was functioning.  (I'm sure all my <a href="http://www.performance-workshop.org/" target="_blank">WOPR</a> friends are cringing right now.)   I basically followed the approach I blogged about on <a href="http://mikebonar.blogspot.ca/2008/01/last-year-i-created-what-i-call-lean.html" target="_blank">value stream mapping and software development</a> except substitute manual functional testing for performance testing.

We used <a href="https://trello.com/about" target="_blank">Trello</a> with great success on this project.  We had one board for the brand release and one board for the full release.  In each board we had a Todo, In Progress, Complete and Parking Lot column.  This was our virtual sticky-note wall and all team members including stakeholders could see at a glance the current state of the project.  Later on we used the same approach for our bug tracking. Trello provides a reporting feature that allowed me to report on <strong>velocity</strong> on a daily basis.  Our 185 tasks were refactored a bit into categories for prioritization, and they were refactored again into smaller tasks once we started gaining momentum.  At production launch we had 173 tasks in the Complete column, 18 In Progress, and 133 in Todo.  There was quite a bit of scope creep over the three months, but all the business critical workflow tasks were completed.  There was a large amount of reporting work that was pushed off until after launch, and this accounted for the bulk of the 133 tasks in Todo.  For forecasting purposes I calculated we were doing around 3.5 tasks/programmer/day or around 17 tasks/week.  For a one-month sprint this would be around 50 tasks/sprint.  Tasks ranged from 1 hour in length for modifications to current application behavior to 1 week for tasks involving new behavior.

For bugs we had at launch 254 Complete, 28 In Progress, and 11 in Todo.  I should point out that we opened the Bug Board at the start of our 3 week  full release UAT phase.  Closing 254 bugs in 3 weeks is an impressive amount of bug squashing in my books, and the best I have ever seen in my career.  At our peak we were closing around 20 bugs per day between 10 programmers.  We were also doing multiple builds per day thanks to some very solid build automation.  Our build times went from 2 days to 2 hours to 20 minutes quite quickly, and this was a key factor in our success.  We had a resource dedicated to builds throughout the entire project.

At no point in time did I tell my stakeholders that we would hit our target deadlines.  Although they wanted solid assurance, I knew that with any software development project things can creep up and bite you.  However, I was able to show that given our task list and our current velocity, reported daily on a wall calendar, that we were on track to being very close to target.  Sounds a bit squishy, I know, but given the velocity numbers and the fact that they could see the changes happening daily (customer is performing functional testing) the management team was quite confident in our approach.

These velocity calculations were the driving measure of the project.  After our first round of hiring, it was easy to see we were not getting the required velocity, so we scaled up our team accordingly.  It was a nervous couple of weeks before the team achieved 20 tasks/week and we had some inkling we could make our deadline.  I was very nervous about being able to find so many able .Net developers on such short notice, and I made it clear to my stakeholders that this was a critical success factor.  I consider myself fortunate to have had the opportunity to work with the team that finally developed.  They were outstanding by any measure.

I have never doubted the agile approach to software development, and I've now been a part of 4 successful agile software development projects.  I am sure an agile software development project can fail for any number of reasons, and no methodology is foolproof.  But, I have never been on an agile project that failed to deliver working software, and so I have a very high confidence level when suggesting this approach to customers.  In my experience, agile is still a very viable approach to delivering software.
