I need to tell my story.  It's a bit shocking, but I feel it is symptomatic of the kinds of things that are plaguing the information technology industry today.  I hope I am wrong about that, but I suspect there are many others who have experienced what I have been through and are suffering in a conspiracy of silence for fear of their jobs.

I've been in IT for over 20 years.  I'm good at what I do.  The bulk of my career was spent working for IBM.  In 2006 I left IBM to go into contracting.  After bouncing around a bit I managed to get my consulting practice going and save enough money to launch a mobile application development start-up.  I've blogged about that here if you have been following my posts.

My start-up launched our application on the Android Market last February.  Unfortunately, it's difficult to make money in the Android Market.  People are not as willing to pay for applications as they are with the iPad market, and you are left with putting advertising in the applications in the hopes of making some revenue.  People are smart; they don't click the ads, and so you don't make a whole lot of money.

To keep things going I decided to take another contracting engagement to supplement my revenue and allow me to continue to hire software developers.  This is where the fun starts.

I was contacted by a small company called JTR Consulting in May of this year to take a Project Manager role for EMC, delivering a virtualization project for Postmedia.  This was a 7 week, fixed price engagement to do the requirements and definition phases of a larger private cloud implementation.

From the very beginning, this project was in trouble.  The EMC team was very disorganized, but as project manager it was my job to sort that out.  The biggest issue was the solution architect they had assigned to the project.  This person was not an IT architect at all but merely a glorified sales person.  Their job was to lead the team in gathering requirements and to define the solution that would be delivered in later phases of the project.  Unfortunately, all this person did was show up once in a while and deliver more sales presentations instead of actually doing the work of an IT Architect.

In the third week of the engagement, the solution architect came in to deliver what should have been a requirements discovery workshop but turned out to be just another sales presentation.  I attended all the workshops, as did the Postmedia VP of Technical Services, our customer sponsor.  I had another call to attend to right after the workshop, and so I slipped out of the meeting just before it ended to get ready for the next call.

The following day I received a note from the Postmedia project manager assigned to manage the Postmedia side if the team asking if I had captured any action items from the workshop.  I had not heard any action items assigned, so I replied that I had not heard any and asked if they had captured any.  I didn't get a response to that query, and later on the Postmedia project manager sent out a broadcast to the entire team stating that from now on they would run all meetings, create all agendas, capture all action items, and that EMC was welcome to come to them first for all project related activities.  As you can imagine, this was quite the shock.

Up to this point I had been running the project by the book.  I am a highly trained project manager, and I had my project control book completely up to date with change logs, risk logs, project plan, steering committee reports, deliverable templates, the works.  Two or three times a day I would touch base with the Postmedia project manager to ensure we were meeting their expectations, and I had been sharing all my project control documents when them as soon as they were ready for release.  As far as I was concerned I was running the perfect project, so I had no idea what had motivated them to make that broadcast announcement effectively cutting me out of the project management loop.

EMC made inquiries with the VP of Technical Services, and they said that they were unhappy with my communication skills.  They also said that I had not been talking to them.

&nbsp;

Let's say you have a person working on your project, and he is a contractor from an external company.  And, let's say you put a knife squarely between his shoulder blades by telling a boldfaced lie about his performance and sending a nasty-gram to his manager without even first speaking to the person.  Would you expect that person to trust you in the future?  Would you expect that person to want to work with you in the future?  What do you think that would do to your reputation in the company or IT community?

Now, let's say that this person decides that this manager doesn't trust him or her now, and  there is no way to continue a sound working relationship on the project.  Let's say that this person also believes that continuing to work on the project will only create more opportunities for this kind of abusive behaviour, and therefore, the best course of action is to leave the project.  Who wins and who loses?

The manager, in this situation, has gotten rid of someone they don't like.  The contractor has gotten themselves out of a bad situation.
