Thanks to Bill Nye for inspiring me to write this.

If you think banning plastic bags is the way to save the planet, I have a quest for you.  Go to the museum, and visit the section from the late 1800's.  This was before the automobile when the primary mode of travel was horse and wagon when there was no such thing as plastic.  And when you are there, ask yourself if the people of that era could fix the following environmental impact.

Before the internet, the only human made thing you could see with the naked eye from space was the clear cut regions of forest in British Columbia Canada.  (Sorry, amazon, we got you beat there.)  Then the internet allowed us to see that we had made an even bigger impact on the planet: Lake Winnipeg had turned green with toxic algae.

Go to a map of Canada, and imagine a big footprint over Lake Winnipeg.  That's our footprint, and it's big.  It's bigger than the plastic islands in the ocean.  It's bigger than any oil spill.  I think you would be hard pressed to find a bigger footprint in the world.  But wait, it gets bigger.

All the fingers pointed to nitrates as the source of the algae bloom, but there just didn't seem to be enough farmland around the border of the lake to account for that much nitrate.  Then David Suzuki,one of Canada's greatest scientists, came out to study the impact.  It turns out there's a logical explanation and a logical fix.  

The river systems that feed Lake Winnipeg stretch all the way to the Alberta border.  When we built all those beautiful prairie roads and ditches to move water off the fields in spring, we unintentionally set up a very efficient system for transporting nitrates into the lake.  The nitrates that would normally be absorbed by nature get transported too quickly to be absorbed by the bald ditches.  Solution: turn the ditches into mini wetlands that slow the flow of water, giving nature  time to absorb it.

Imagine the map of Canada now, but with the footprint stretching from Lake Winnipeg all the way to the Alberta border.  You could do the math on how many miles of road that would be.  They are called "mile roads" because farms were originally measured in 1 square mile sections.  Canada had, at its peak, around 350,000 farms, two thirds of which would have been laid out across the prairies.  1 square mile of farm typically has 4 miles of ditch around it.  That's a lot of ditches.

Suzuki's work also showed that the dozen hydroelectric dams that ring the lake prevent the water levels from rising and falling naturally with the seasons.  This has caused one of the largest, healthiest wetlands on the planet to go silent.  Solution: modify a dozen hydroelectric dams to let the water levels go up and down again with the seasons.  Build a bunch of wind farms to offset the loss in fiscal revenue when Manitoba Hydro let's the water levels drop.

Notice how this solution has nothing to do with carbon taxes or banning plastic bags.  I think this brings into stark relief just how weak our climate change management strategies are today.  You can raise the price of gasoline as high as you want, but Lake Winnipeg will still be green.  If you have an environmental platform that doesn't include fixing the largest footprint in Canada, you don't have a credible platform.  You can force citizens to stop using plastic bags, but that lake will still be green.  You can force citizens to drive electric cars, but that wetland will still be silent.  

The one thing these broken strategies have in common is that they expect the consumer to pay to save the planet.  Before we start arguing about Keynes, Friedman and Galbraith, I want to point out the fundamental facts about the Canadian economy.  In Canada, the largest customer in the marketplace is the Federal Government.  And, in each province in Canada, the largest customer in the marketplace is the Provincial government.  Our country is big, and our population is small; if we don't use our tax dollars, we simply cannot achieve anything at scale.

I think you are starting to get the picture that fixing this would be the biggest mega-project in the history of Canada.  How in the world are we going to pay for this?  And who's going to pay for it?  This footprint stretches across all our borders.

So what if?  What if we, the people of Canada (and I mean the First Nations of Canada and the People of Canada), decide to place a royalty on oil flowing down our pipelines.  And, what if we took that money (upwards of $20 million per day at current production rates), and used it to fund an expanded role for Parks Canada and our Provincial Parks systems.  Instead of forcing the oil industry through environmental reviews, we use our royalty money to build/restore/preserve healthy ecosystems around our footprints.  What if we made terra-forming healthy ecosystems a natural part of how we build infrastructure in Canada?  

As Suzuki showed, only the healthiest ecosystems can successfully withstand our footprints.  Weakened ecosystems lose their ability to act as buffers between us and the planet.  Ideally, if we build a school or a road or a pipeline we should be surrounding them with parkland.  And, with the oil industry money machine driving restoration efforts, it would not be long before we are healing the planet faster than we are hurting it.  That's what Hawking meant when he said we may be out of time; we are leaving footprints faster than the planet can cover them.  We need to get in the game.

Think about it.
