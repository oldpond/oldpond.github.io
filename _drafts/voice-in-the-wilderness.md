So, I live on the west shore of Vancouver Island now. I'm a west-coaster. It's beautiful out here. Every day I shake my head and wonder at how lucky I am to be out here. I bought a garden. I know how much work beautiful gardens are to create, and I've never really had the time. It's a great place to sit and  contemplate the future. I'm in real danger of being retired. ;)

Back in the 90's, I was involved in the data center automation craze. I say "craze" because even the information technology industry has fads and fashions. I was quite good at automation, and I had great mainframe tools to work with while I was automating the data center at one of Canada's largest financial institutions. I also had a copy of <strong>You Can't Work in the Dark!: Understanding Automated Data Center Operations </strong>by George Kurtz.  The message delivered in this book was a bit contradictory to what the big vendors were selling at the time.  They were preaching lights out, and as George indicated, that's just not reality.  People run data centers; they don't run themselves.  This was the first IT fad that I can point to in my experience with the IT industry.

Can you imagine telling your enterprise customers that application "silos are bad" and you should design toward integration.  That's what they big guys were selling back in the late 90's early 2000's.  As a result, application designs were tightly integrated either in the application layer or the database layer.  Architects designed systems on layers like communication and printing layers that where shared across applications.  Databases where shamelessly available to all comers with views and triggers passing back and forth between business domains like a door to door salesman.  Fast forward to today, and customers find they have to upgrade these aging systems.  The big problem facing them now is that all the applications that share the platform architecture have to be upgraded at the same time.  They are finding that you can't upgrade one part of the business domain without breaking another part.  What customers want now is a loosely coupled application architect that provides flexibility to the enterprise.  For example, they want to upgrade the supply chain management system, but leave the retail operations system alone.  They want their silos back, and that makes perfect sense to me.

Part of the problem is that today's generation of IT leadership in Canada is often unwilling to admit they bought a lemon.  "Tight integration" is a <strong>technology </strong> just like "loosely coupled" is a technology, and those that bought tight integration are having a little buyers remorse right now.  Those applications that they built around 2001 are at end of life, and customers are facing big replacement projects, not just legacy refresh projects.  The vendors who sold tight integration have a bit of egg on their faces now, and their credibility has suffered because of it.  As an enterprise customer, do you go to the guy who sold you the lemon in the first place and ask them to build you another one?  Or do you look for that lone voice in the wilderness who believes there's a better way?

Which brings us to the current crisis.  Nobody wants to work in this industry anymore besides those that are currently in it.  Plumbers have better jobs than IT practitioners.
