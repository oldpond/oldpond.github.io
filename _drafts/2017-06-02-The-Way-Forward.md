First, a little background.  I think I joined the Manitoba Naturalists Society when I was around 14.  I recall they had a very nice newsletter.  By the time I'd finished high school I had completed three hiking trips to the Rockies.  And I have spent my life hiking, canoeing, climbing, and skiing around the prairies and the Canadian Shield, and now Victoria where, which has been home to my family for the past 3 years.  

Some time around 1980, my current wife, then girlfriend, and I, tried to start the Green Party of Manitoba.  I remember creating a newsletter with this funky guy from California who claimed the army had burned his marijuana farm.  The only ones who showed up to the first meeting were the three of us, Nick Ternette, and one of Nick's buddies who looked like he was in his seventies.  I would have been around 19 or 20 then, and Nick was way to radical for me.  So, I fell out of politics and had a life in IT.

Fast forward to the Internet age.  Google posts photos of Lake Winnipeg from satellites, and the lake is green. We all knew the lake wasn't that good to swim in anymore, but now we could see from space that the entire lake was filled with algae. And, it keeps happening year after year.

Imagine my happiness when I hear David Suzuki's voice talking about Lake Winnipeg!  I was so happy that one of Canada's greatest scientists had put his mind to work on the problem.  Sure enough, as I listened, I was not disappointed.  Dr. Suzuki confirmed what we all thought about nitrates being a factor.  What I learned that day was that the watershed for Lake Winnipeg stretches all the way to the Alberta border.  I also learned that by building that amazing network of beautiful farm roads that stretches all the way to the rockies, we weakened the ecosystem's ability to absorb nitrates, the very thing that makes things green.  

I also learned that we had stopped up all the exits from Lake Winnipeg with hydro electric dams.  This caused one of the largest wetlands on the plant to stop breathing.  Wetlands require seasonal fluctuation of water levels to survive.  You can't see that from space.  You have to go there and listen to the silence.

The good news is, Dr. Suzuki talked about solutions.  You could put up alternative energy systems like wind farms to supplement the hydro supply and allow the lake to rise and fall again.  You could turn all those beautiful prairie roads into nuisance-flooding-friendly roads and wetlands that slow the rate of runoff in the spring, giving the land more time to absorb the nitrates before they reach the lake.

Great, I thought!  Let's do this!  We can calculate how much work this will be, and how much it will cost, and how long it will take to complete.  And, you begin to realize just how big this impact that we made really is. If you've ever dug a row in your garden or a field with a tractor or a ditch with heavy equipment, you can get a sense that if you had 100,000 square miles of roads to repair by hand it would take a very long time and a large army of laborers.  On the other hand, if you had a small army of teams with heavy equipment it would be much faster, but this is a project on a scale of building 10 hydroelectric dams.  

That addresses the drainage issue.  What about restarting the wetland with wind farms.  Wind farms are also the realm of heavy industry, so we can probably just double the number of heavy equipment teams we have in our mental estimates, and come to the realization that this is a very large, very expensive fix.

Who's going to pay for this?  That's the first question today's voter is going to ask.  These are the same citizens who are having trouble paying the mortgage, and whose governments are running deficits.  Private industry isn't going to donate that kind of money to the environment.  It's an interesting problem, and one that often kills any good intentions we may have towards the environment.

Well, I know from living on the prairies, that we have a healthy petrochemical industry out there.  I also know that the there's no shortage of supply in the tar sands.  I also know that the price of oil has made that industry viable.  I also know, we've all seen how much money comes out of those wells and flows down those pipelines day in and day out, year after year.  

It's not hard to calculate the royalties required to fully fund the restoration of Lake Winnipeg and still have a very healthy petrochemical industry.  The new Kinder Morgan pipeline is expected to carry 890,000 barrels per day.  Do the math.  It's in the $Billions.  Imagine having billions of dollars PER YEAR to restore wetlands, build nuisance-flooding-friendly roads, put up wind farms, hire more scientists, give every homeless kid a job in parks and recreation...  I'm dreaming, I know.

What I know is this.  Humans are going to impact the environment, even when they don't mean to.  If we want to build a school, then the best way to mitigate the impact of that on the planet, is to put a park around it.  Parkland being to me that wonderfully healthy ecosystem you see in non-impacted areas like the ones our national parks hold for us.  I don't mean a few pieces of sod and a few spruce trees planted.

I also know we are past the stage where banning plastic bags has any chance of turning the tide on our impacts.  We need to be world leaders in recycling and water treatment and forestry and fisheries and building clean industries surrounded by the healthiest parkland we can afford to create, sustain or restore.  These are heavy industries, and they currently depend on a healthy oil industry to operate.  They require a great deal of resources to achieve.  In Canada the largest customer in the market is the Federal government.  And, in each province, the largest customer in the market is the Provincial Government.  If our governments do not support a healthy petrochemical industry as a direct source of funding for these ministries, I do not see how Canadians can achieve the scale required to provide hope of staying ahead of our own footprint.

It seems odd to me that we would expect the oil industry, which is very good at extracting petrochemicals, to be good at creating parkland.  Canadians have always expected their governments to manage parkland and the environment.  It makes sense that we should build these terraforming-like industries within our government industries.  Why wouldn't want the largest customer in the market throwing their weight behind these kinds of jobs and this kind of future?

If you think about it, putting this kind of healthy ecosystem around every impact we make on this planet is the absolute best we could do to protect the planet from our impacts.  I know it seems strange to say you want a pipeline in a park because nobody wants a spill in a park.  But we know that the healthier the ecosystem the more able it is to sustain our impact.  Think about what that would look like.  No matter if it was a school, or a road, or a pipeline, if we surrounded it from end to end in healthy parkland, we would be giving the planet it's best chance to survive our impacts.

In other words, imagine if we tapped into the oil industry and put a park around the entire oil industry and laid parkland all up and down those pipelines.  We'd have a very nice Cross Canada Trail, and we'd have a much healthier environment, and and we'd have a cleaner oil industry.  Our First Nations friends and neighbors would of course get all the same royalties and benefits, and a future of good jobs, healthy places to live for our families, and a healthy planet all start to look like they are within our grasp.  

I believe this is a way forward for our peoples and our planet.  

Let's drop down into the here and now.  In British Columbia Canada, a new government is forming right now.  It's forming out of friendship between the BC NDP and the BC Greens.  It's forming out of people who work in heavy industries and people who want to heal the planet.  In Canada, we have a tradition of peace, order and good government.  We have choices to make, choices about how we spend our tax dollars.  I say we spend them on making Canada the world leader in environment management.  

It's moments like these where the human race has an opportunity to adapt.  We can choose to put our governments and our resources to work on healing this planet.  The oil industry had the resource to heal itself and the planet, but they're not in that business.  This is why we pay taxes and have good governments; so we can provide share services like parks, waster and water management, forestry management, fisheries management, and natural resource management, on a scale can that has a chance slowing our impacts on the planet.  Or, we could argue while the ice caps melt.

The reason I left the Green Party platform behind all those years ago, is that the platform is too lopsided.  It's easy for greens to argue against the oil industry because of the visibly obvious affects of fossil fuels.  But, they never mention all the things that would disappear without a healthy petrochemical industry.  Things like modern medicine, air travel, cell phones and computers, and paved roads.  I believe killing the oil industry is not the correct approach.  Harnessing the oil industry is.  It's going to take a lot of science and technology to fix the planet.  Electric cars are here to stay now, and one day they will replace the combustion engine.  But, those pipelines will continue to flow, even after the old gas guzzlers are gone, if we intend to maintain our current lifestyle.










The big question is, would it be fast enough?
