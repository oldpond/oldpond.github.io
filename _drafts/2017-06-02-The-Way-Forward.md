First, a little background.  I think I joined the Manitoba Naturalists Society when I was around 14.  I recall they had a very nice newsletter.  By the time I'd finished high school I had completed three hiking trips to the Rockies.  And I have spent my life hiking, canoeing, climbing, and skiing around the prairies and the Canadian Shield, and now Victoria where, which has been home to my family for the past 3 years.  

Some time around 1980, my current wife then girlfriend and I, tried to start the Green Party of Manitoba.  I remember creating a newsletter with this funky guy from California who claimed the army had burned his marijuana farm.  The only ones who showed up to the first meeting were the three of us, Nick Ternette, and one of his buddies who looked like he was in his seventies.  I would have been around 19 or 20 then, and Nick was way to radical for me.  So, I fell out of politics and had a life in IT.

Fast forward to the Internet age.  Google posts photos of Lake Winnipeg from satellites, and the lake is green. We all knew the lake wasn't that good to swim in anymore, but now we could see from space that the entire lake was filled with algae. And, it keeps happening year after year.

Imagine my happiness when I hear David Suzuki's voice talking about Lake Winnipeg!  I was so happy that one of Canada's greatest scientists had put his mind to work on the problem.  Sure enough, as I listened, I was not disappointed.  Dr. Suzuki confirmed what we all thought about nitrates being a factor.  What I learned that day was that the watershed for Lake Winnipeg stretches all the way to the Alberta border.  I also learned that by building that amazing network of beautiful farm roads that stretches all the way to the rockies, we weakened the ecosystem's ability to absorb nitrates, the very thing that makes them green.  

I also learned that we had stopped up all the exits from Lake Winnipeg with hydro electric dams.  This caused one of the largest wetlands on the plant to stop breathing.  Wetlands require seasonal fluctuation of water levels to survive.  You can't see that from space.  You have to go there and listen to the silence.

The good news is, Dr. Suzuki talked about solutions.  You could put up alternative energy systems like wind farms to supplement the hydro supply and allow the lake to rise and fall again.  You could turn all those beautiful prairie roads into nuisance-flooding-friendly roads and wetlands that slow the rate of runoff to the lake in the spring, giving the land more time to absorb the nitrates before they reach the lake.

Great, I thought!  Let's do this!  We can calculate how much work this will be, and how much it will cost, and how long it will take to complete.  And, you begin to realize just how big this impact that we made really is. If you've ever dug a row in your garden or a field with a tractor or a ditch with heavy equipment, you can kind of get a sense that if you had 100,000 square miles of roads to repair by hand it would take a very long time and a large army of laborers.  On the other hand, if you did a small army of teams with heavy equipment it would be much faster but it would be a project more on the scale of building 10 hydroelectric dams.  

That addresses the drainage issue.  What about restarting the wetland with wind farms.  Wind farms are also the realm of heavy industry, so we can probably just double the number of heavy equipment teams we have in our mental estimates, and come to the realization that this is a very large, very expensive fix.

Who's going to pay for this?  That's the first question today's voter is going to ask.  These are the same citizens who are having trouble paying the mortgage.  They don't want the kind of tax increase it would take to fix this.  Governments are all broke and barely keeping our shared services going.  Private industry isn't going to donate that kind of money to the environment.  It's an interesting problem, and one that often kills any good intentions we may have towards the environment.

Well, I know from living on the prairies, that we have a healthy petrochemical industry out there.  I also know that the there's no shortage of supply in the tar sand.  I also know that the price of oil has made that industry viable again.  I also know, we've all see how much money comes out of those wells each year.  

It's not hard to calculate the royalties required to fully fund the restoration of Lake Winnipeg and still have a very healthy petrochemical industry.  The new Kinder Morgan pipeline is expected to carry 890,000 barrels per day.  Do the math.  It's in the $Billions.  Imagine having billions of dollars PER YEAR to restore wetlands, build nuisance-flooding-friendly roads, put up wind farms, hire more scientists, give every homeless kid a job in parks and recreation.  I'm dreaming, I know.

My big takeaway from Dr. Suzuki's work on Lake Winnipeg was this.  Humans are going to impact the environment, even when they don't mean to.  If we want to build a school, then the best way to mitigate the impact of that on the planet, is to put a park around it.  Parkland being to me that wonderfully healthy ecosystem you see in non-impacted areas like our national parks hold for us.  I don't mean a few pieces of sod and a few spruce trees.

Putting this kind of healthy ecosystem around every impact we make on this planet is the absolute best we could do, if we had the kind of money that a healthy petrochemical industry could provide.  Think about what that would look like.  No matter if it was a school, or a road, or a pipeline, if we surrounded it from end to end in healthy parkland, we would be giving the planet it's best chance to survive our impacts.

In other words, imagine if we tapped into the oil industry and put a park around the entire oil industry and laid parkland all up and down those pipelines.  We'd have a very nice Cross Canada Trail, and we'd have a much healthier environment, and oil industry would be happy, and the taxpayer would be happy.  










The big question is, would it be fast enough?
