First, a little background.  I think I joined the Manitoba Naturalists Society when I was around 14.  I recall they had a very nice newsletter.  By the time I'd finished high school I had completed three hiking trips to the Rockies.  I have spent my life hiking, canoeing, climbing, and skiing around the prairies and the Canadian Shield, and now Victoria, home to my family for the past 3 years.  

Some time around 1980 I tried to start the Green Party of Manitoba.  I remember creating a newsletter with this funky guy from California who claimed the army had burned his marijuana farm.  The only ones who showed up to the first meeting were the three of us, Nick Ternette, and one of Nick's buddies who looked like he was in his seventies.  I would have been around 19 or 20 then, and Nick was way too radical for me.  So, I fell out of politics and had a life in IT.

Fast forward to the Internet age.  Google posts photos of Lake Winnipeg from satellites, and the lake is green. We all knew the lake wasn't that good to swim in anymore, but now we could see from space that the entire lake was filled with algae. And, it keeps happening year after year.

Imagine my happiness when I hear David Suzuki's voice talking about Lake Winnipeg!  I was so happy one of Canada's greatest scientists had put his mind to the problem.  Dr. Suzuki confirmed what we all thought about nitrates being a factor.  What I learned that day was that the watershed for Lake Winnipeg stretches all the way to the Alberta border.  I also learned that by building that amazing network of beautiful farm roads that stretches all the way to the rockies, we weakened the ecosystem's ability to absorb the nitrates we use to grow our food.  

I also learned that we had stopped up all the exits from Lake Winnipeg with hydro electric dams.  This caused one of the largest wetlands on the plant to stop breathing. You can't see that from space.  You have to go there and listen to the silence.

The good news is, Dr. Suzuki talked about solutions.  We could put up alternative energy systems like wind farms to supplement the hydro supply and allow the lake to rise and fall again.  We could turn all those beautiful prairie roads into nuisance-flooding-friendly roads and wetlands that slow the rate of runoff in the spring, giving the land more time to absorb the nitrates before they reach the lake.

If you've ever dug a row in your garden, or a field with a tractor, or a ditch with heavy equipment, you can get a sense that if you had 100,000 square miles of roads to repair by hand it would take a very long time and a large army of laborers.  This is a very big fix.  

Who's going to pay for this?  That's the first question today's voter is going to ask.  These are the same voters who are having trouble paying the mortgage, and whose governments are running deficits.  Private industry isn't going to donate that kind of money to the environment.  Only governments can achieve this kind of scale.

I know from living on the prairies that we have a healthy petrochemical industry out there.  I also know that the there's no shortage of supply in the tar sands.  I also know that the price of oil has made that industry viable.  We've all seen how much money comes out of those wells and flows down those pipelines day in and day out, year after year.  

It's not hard to calculate the royalties required to fully fund the restoration of Lake Winnipeg and still have a very healthy petrochemical industry.  The new Kinder Morgan pipeline is expected to carry 890,000 barrels per day.  Do the math.  It's in the $Billions.  Imagine having billions of dollars PER YEAR to restore wetlands, build nuisance-flooding-friendly roads, put up wind farms, hire more scientists, give every homeless kid a job in parks and recreation...  I'm dreaming. :)

I believe this is a way forward for our peoples and our planet.
