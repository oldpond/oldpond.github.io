I've been in the IT industry for nearly 30 years now.  The first half of my career I spent working with what I'll call the larger, slower enterprises.  These are the shops that run very large projects of 30 or more people costing tens, if not hundreds of millions of dollars to deliver.  They are characterized by heavy methodologies that produce a lot of deliverables.  These methodologies are designed to reduce risk, however they do not always succeed in delivering high customer satisfaction.

The second half I've spent watching the smaller, faster enterprises.  These are the shops that can deliver value early on in the development cycle so that stakeholders can see where their investment is heading.  These are all the startups that managed to grab big chunks of the new internet marketplace.  These guys are winning, and one of the reasons is that they use smaller, faster methodologies, like the one from Andy Hunt and team called the [Grows Method](http://growsmethod.com/).  If you saw my blog about [Agility](http://oldpond.github.io/agile/agility/software/2015/09/16/experience-report-agile-is-not-dead.html), you will recall we used a method very similar to [Grows](http://growsmethod.com/) with great success.  I would like to share my experience with both worlds in the hope that stakeholders of all sizes can get more satisfaction from their IT dollar.

The last thing that happens after a heavy methodology runs "off the rails" is a learning session.  You list the things you did right, the things you did wrong, and the things you could do better next time.

For the purposes of a simple learning exercise, imagine that you are going to perform the learning session at the beginning of the project instead of at the end as in the heavy methodology.  An amazing thing happens when you perform this simple little test.  It provides you with a crystal ball into the future of what you do not want to happen to your project.  I will describe a fictional learning session with a fictional customer who chooses a heavy methodology to deliver a Common off the Shelf (COTS) solution.  If you are not familiar with COTS solutions, they are packages that you install, configure, and run.  They do not require software development, but often many data moving tools will be written.  Think of personal financial management software on steroids.

Chrystal Ball: You were really good at methodology.  You had lots of deliverables.  Everyone in the IT industry knows this list: project plans, work breakdown structures, issue logs, decision logs, requirements documents, process diagrams, business requirements documents, functional specification documents, technical specification documents, data flow diagrams, lots and lots of ETL jobs, architecture diagrams, sequence diagrams, and weekly meetings for all these deliverables plus daily stand-ups, and of course, estimates.  There are even some things from more Agile approaches like lightweight ticketing systems and backlogs.  In other words, you added some "agile" things to your heavy methodology to make it more "agile"/heavy. You had all these things, and you did them very well.  

Chrystal Ball: You were really good at process.  You followed your methodology meticulously.  Unfortunately, Murphy's Law strikes, the worst case scenario happens, and you do not have a working system.  Stakeholders are concerned about expectations not met.  

Now, for learning purposes, let me describe something we call a critical business driver in the IT business.  These are the most critical outcomes for the business users in any project.  They are a bit tricky to define because the list can grow rapidly and get "fuzzy" very quickly when you just start listing all the things that are critical.  They are best described as one or two things that if not done right, make all the other things you did well not matter one bit.  Critical business drivers are important to define early in the project because they really focus the team on the target.

In our case, the target is working software.  If I were to describe this in LEAN terms, working software is the one thing the customer is standing on the loading dock waiting for.  Your project may give the customer a whole bunch of very nice deliverables, but if your project does not deliver working software you have failed.  What follows is a common pattern in IT: The big, long list of deliverables becomes a big, long list of excuses for why the delays are occurring.  "Where's my software?"  "Oh, we're still working on XYZ deliverable" becomes the project mantra.  This sets off alarm bells with the stakeholders.

Chrystal Ball: Unfortunately, nobody at this point suspects that the methodology may be contributing to the delays.

This is a cultural issue in many shops.  They will not question methodology midstream.  This is why a smaller, faster method like [Grows](http://growsmethod.com/) stresses the ability to adapt.  Failure to adapt here will likely lead you to set off down the same path with the same heavy methodology that got you this far. You will very likely end up producing new versions of the exact same deliverables.  You might produce the deliverables a little faster the second time because you solved a lot of problems the first time through.  This might lead you to think you are getting better at processes and lead to a false sense of momentum on the team, when, in fact, all you might succeed in doing is produce more of what the customer is NOT asking for, deliverables, and LESS of what the customer is asking for: working software.  

At this point a learning session would carry on describing all the things you did poorly. These things are best left to quiet contemplation.  

Now the fun part.  Let's imagine the same situation if we had applied a smaller, faster method that had one deliverable: working software.  What might that method look like.  Let's imagine the heavy method took one 9-months sprint, and one 3-month sprint, and did not produce working software.  That means our lighter, faster method has to produce working software at least once in one year to be 100% more successful than the heavy method.  Sounds like an easy target.

These big, enterprise systems, as we all know, are not like installing your personal financial software on your desktop.  They are complex systems with a lot of moving parts. Just to make the math easy, let's say it takes 3 weeks to install, configure, and get running your typical enterprise class COTS solution.  This includes getting the hardware up, the software installed and configured, users logging in and performing some sort of business scenario.  So, sprint one in our new lighter, faster method is 3 weeks.  The goal of sprint one is aways to get something working.

Chrystal Ball: Murphy's Law strikes! The smaller, faster team finds it can't get the old data into the new system.  After 3 weeks you managed to install, configure and run the COTS solution, but without data you have no working software.

In other words, the smaller, faster team found out about the problem after only 3 weeks, not 9 months as in the case of the heavy methodology. That means the smaller, faster method has a new target.  It now has 12 3-week sprints to produce working software if it wants to be 100% better than the heavy methodology.  A good math exercise right now would be to calculate the probability of success if we just selected random solutions 12 times.

Now, there are a couple of ways to go when you have a data problem like this.  You can put a lot of people to work on complex data moving tools, and this is often the first choice because we're computer people and it's data, right?  It even sounds smaller and faster because you can throw away the old data moving tools after you get a good set of data in the new system.  It's an easy choice and if it works you are heroes.  But, what if it doesn't work?  What if you struggle for nine months trying to build complex data moving tools, and it still doesn't work?

The other way to go is with manual data entry.  It's not hard to estimate how much data a small team of data entry specialists can produce.  At one record per minute with 5 hours of productive entry time per day that's 300 records per day.  A team of ten could produce 3000 records per day or 30000 records in two weeks.  That means if we want to adjust our sprints with manual data entry, we would have, assuming no overlap, 5-week sprints comprised of 3 weeks of install, configure, and run, followed by two weeks of data entry.  Since we have already burned up one sprint, we have about 10 5-week sprints left in our one year target.  

Another good math exercise here would be to see if there's a change in probability of success between the two iterations of our smaller, faster method.  We have 12 random tries in the first iteration followed by 11 random tries in the second.  Interesting question: what if the data entry is cumulative?  The good news is the new manual data entry team's probability of success is still 11 time higher than the heavy methodology team that chose to write complex data moving tools.

I think at this point you can wee where I'm going with this.  But, let's do one more.  We know that one of the characteristics of smaller, faster methods is their emphasis on velocity.  A modern, software development shop using lightweight continuous integration and build automation, the DEVOPS world, can show the customer working software multiple times per day.  This is what the [Grows Method](http://growsmethod.com/) means when it stresses "tracer bullet development".  Get something working.  Then adapt and grow.

The reason why sprint one is always "get something working" is because it brings you to a decision point very early on in the cycle.  This provides the team with very valuable opportunities to adapt before a lot of effort is built up.  It provides an opportunity to say, if we didn't get it right last time, what are we doing differently this time that makes us think we're going to get a different result?  This is what "velocity" is all about in smaller, faster methods.  The faster we can get this "positive feedback loop" of working software to customer going, the faster we can adapt our solution to the customer's needs.

In other words, let's say we decided to write our COTS solution from scratch using a modern language and platform of our choice, and we used 3-week sprints with daily build cycles, then we would have at least 365 chances to get it right with our even newer, smaller, faster method compared to our heavy method.  In other words, we could go from 0 to 11 chances to 365 chances to get it right in under one year.  You could draw a nice graph of that curve.  A final exercise might be to add data entry to this software development approach and ask the question again, what if the data entry was cumulative.

Here's the secret of these smaller, faster methods: their high velocity allows them to build a working solution after one sprint, and then simply "grow" functionality onto your working software at a very high rate.  High velocity means high probability of success.  Who doesn't want that?
